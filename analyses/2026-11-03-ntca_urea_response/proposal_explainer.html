The idea in brief

NtcA is the main nitrogen "alarm" regulator in cyanobacteria. When cells run short of nitrogen, NtcA switches on genes for taking up and assimilating other nitrogen sources, and usually turns up its own gene too. [interpretation]

Urea is a nitrogen source that cells split into ammonium inside the cell. So one expectation is that cells growing well on urea are not nitrogen-stressed, and NtcA would stay quiet. The other possibility is that urea is harder to use, so the cells feel mildly short of nitrogen and NtcA goes up. [interpretation]

So "does NtcA respond?" is really asking: do urea-grown cells look nitrogen-stressed?

The data we have

The knowledge graph has one study that fits: Tolonen et al. 2006 (Mol Syst Biol), which measured gene expression on microarrays in two strains. [KG]

The control answers one question: if NtcA changes, can these arrays see it? If NtcA didn't rise under outright starvation, a "no change" on urea would mean nothing.

NtcA's gene ID is PMM0246 in MED4 and PMT1831 in MIT9313. We use IDs because gene names can be ambiguous.

Reading it plainly

  • The control works. Under nitrogen removal, NtcA is up strongly in both strains (up to about 22× in MED4 at 6 h and about 12× in MIT9313 at 12 h), and the paper calls it significant.
  • MIT9313 on urea: slightly up (+0.86, about 1.8×), not significant. This looks like a real "no clear change".
  • MED4 on urea is the puzzle. NtcA is down about 4.8× (−2.27), the 6th-largest change of 1,696 genes, yet the paper did not call it significant. A change that big is usually called, so the replicate arrays probably disagreed with each other. The MED4 0 h starvation point shows a similar uncalled drop (−2.17).

Why a big change can still be "not significant"

How we'll read the result

We wrote these readings down before looking further. That stops us from fitting a story to whatever comes out.

If we see……we'll say
NtcA and at least 2 of its targets (glnA, amt1, glnB) significantly up on ureaUrea-grown cells look nitrogen-stressed. This would overturn our expectation.
NtcA not called, small changeNo detectable change: a bounded negative, valid because the control shows the arrays can detect changes
NtcA not called, large change (like MED4)"Large change measured, not called significant." That is not the same as "no change", and we won't report it as one.
NtcA and its targets downUrea cells may be less N-stressed than ammonium cells
The two strains go in opposite directionsReported strain by strain. Not counted as evidence either way.

Two safety checks

Target genes

NtcA's own gene can be noisy. Its targets show whether NtcA is actually doing anything. They must go up under starvation. If they don't, our data pull is broken.

Ribosome genes (should not move)

If the ribosome genes all shift together on urea, the cultures were growing at different speeds. Then any NtcA change could just be a growth-rate effect, not a nitrogen effect.

One limit to keep in mind: the urea cultures were sampled once, after they had fully adjusted. We can't see whether NtcA jumped briefly when the cells were first switched to urea.

Choices for you

NtcA alone, or NtcA plus its target genes?
Recommend: include the targets (glnA, glnB, amt1, urtA, ureC, pipX). Without them, the noisy MED4 result can't be read.
How to report a big change the paper didn't call significant
Recommend: as its own category, "large change, not called significant", kept separate from "no change".
Use the ribosome genes as the "should not move" check?
Recommend: yes, judged on whether their changes shift together, not only on how many are called significant.
Scope
Recommend: urea only. Cyanate, nitrite and coculture data exist in the knowledge graph but would take this past the 10-minute exercise.
